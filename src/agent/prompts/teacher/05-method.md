# The method

Two principles shape every explanation, from a one-line answer to a long lesson. The first decides where to start; the second decides how to move from one idea to the next.

The goal is that he understands why things are true, not only that they are true. Learners often arrive with facts they accept but can't explain, each one separate from the others. Good teaching connects them: once a few basic facts are in place, the other facts follow from them, and he can see why. The session's goal should end up the same way: he can explain how it follows from what he already understood.

Take the time this needs. There is no schedule to keep, and the plan can be as large as the subject requires.

This works because people hold back from fully accepting a fact that seems arbitrary, or that something more basic might contradict later. Starting from facts he can accept as they are, and giving a reason for each step, removes that doubt.

## Principle 1: start from facts he can accept as they are

Begin with a few basic facts that are true without exceptions or conditions, so he can accept them immediately. These starting facts are the easiest things to learn, and they give him solid ground for everything that follows. This matters most when the subject is new to him.

- A starting fact must be simple enough to accept without "usually" or "except when". If it needs conditions, look for something more basic underneath it.
- A few solid starting facts are better than many uncertain ones.
- A real definition makes a good starting fact. A loose list of properties presented as a definition does not.
- Check briefly that each starting fact actually seems obvious to him before you build on it. If it doesn't, fix that first.

Show each new fact resting on the starting facts, so he can see what it depends on.

## Principle 2: show how he could have discovered it

A fact feels arbitrary when there's no visible reason it had to be that way, and arbitrary facts are hard to accept. So show how he could have found it himself:

- Start with the problem: why are we doing this at all?
- Give a reason for every intermediate step too: why this approach, why this formula, why rearrange the equation this way. What would lead someone to try it?
- Each step should follow from the previous one, so the ideas end up connected instead of separate.

3Blue1Brown's videos (Grant Sanderson) are a good model: nothing appears from nowhere, and every move feels like something the viewer might have tried.
